Organic farming in Australia

Australia, the organic giant

Australia has more certified organic farmland than any other country, about half of the world's total. This page follows that land from the paddock to the port to the supermarket checkout, and asks whether Australians' own appetite for organic food has kept pace.

Part 1

Australia's organic boom

1aAustralia's certified organic farmland has grown more than fourfold

In 2004, 12.1 million hectares of Australian farmland were certified organic. By 2023 it was 53.0 million, more than four times as much and about 15% of all the country's farmland.

The same total was reported for every year from 2017 to 2022, which suggests the figure was not updated in those years. In 2026 the Department of Agriculture revised its estimate to 44.4 million hectares using a new method. This page uses the UN FAO series so that years and countries can be compared fairly.

1bThe world's organic land grew steadily, Australia's grew in two leaps

Of all the organic land added worldwide since 2004, 53% was in Australia: 41 of 77 million new hectares. But Australia grew in two big leaps, in 2013–2017 and 2022–2023, while the rest of the world grew steadily. So Australia's share of the world total fell to 27% in 2013 before climbing back to 54%.

Part 2

Australia, the organic giant

2a15% of Australia's farmland is now certified organic, up from 3% in 2004

As a share of its farmland, Australia (14.6%) ranks 13th in the world, behind countries such as Austria (27%) and Uruguay (25%). Drag the slider to watch organic farming spread since 2004.

2bAustralia alone has about half of the world's organic farmland

Measured in hectares, however, Australia has no rival. Its 53.0 million hectares are more than the next 14 countries combined.

2c54 of every 100 organic hectares on Earth are Australian

More than half of all the certified organic farmland on Earth is in Australia. Its 53.0 million hectares are more than the next 14 countries combined.

2dThe land is in the north and centre, the operators in the south-east

That land is spread very unevenly. In the Northern Territory, Tasmania and the ACT there are about 136,000 hectares of organic land for every certified operator. In Victoria there are fewer than 100.

Part 3

From paddock to port

Australian organic products are worth about AU$1.02 billion a year: AU$658 million spent in Australian shops and AU$364 million in exports. But the businesses behind it are feeling the squeeze.

3aFreight is the biggest worry for processors, labour for farmers

Almost four in five organic processors say freight and logistics costs are putting pressure on their business. Moving food across a country this big is expensive.

For farmers, the cost of labour comes first (65%), closely followed by energy and fuel (63%).

3bTwo-thirds of Australia's organic exports go to the United States

Only a small number of these businesses sell overseas: 13% of producers and 14% of processors export. But the organic food that does leave Australia mostly goes to one place.

3cMeat makes up almost two-thirds of Australia's organic exports

And most of what leaves is meat. Beef sent to the United States alone accounts for 26,483 tonnes, more than half of everything Australia exports as organic.

Part 4

At the checkout

Australians spent AU$657.6 million on organic groceries in 2024/25, 11.9% more than the year before. That sounds like booming demand, but it isn't the whole story.

4aHigher prices explain most of the AU$70 million rise in organic sales

The average price of organic food per kilogram rose by 9.2% in a year. Most of the extra money spent went on paying more, not on buying more.

The number of households buying organic grew only a little (+1.8%), and the share of households buying it at all slipped from 77.2% to 76.8%.

4bFrozen fruit and fresh meat surged, chilled juice sales halved

Fresh vegetables are the biggest of these categories and still growing (+17%). Frozen fruit and desserts grew fastest (+46%).

Other habits are fading: organic chilled juice sales halved, and ice cream, milk and infant food all fell.

4cFour in five organic grocery dollars are spent at the major supermarkets

Most of this shopping happens in the same places as everyday groceries. Specialty and independent stores, online shops and farmers selling direct take only a small slice.

4dPrice is still the biggest barrier to buying organic, though it eased slightly

Price is by far the main reason Australians give for not buying more organic food. Among people who never buy it, more than nine in ten say it costs too much.

Trust is the second barrier, and it has not improved. One in three organic buyers has unknowingly bought a product that wasn't really certified organic. Australia is the only OECD country without a national standard for the word "organic".

In short

A giant abroad, a slow climber at home

Australia farms about half of the world's certified organic land. More than a third of the industry's sales by value are exports, and two-thirds of those exports by weight go to the United States, mostly as beef.

At home, Australians are spending more on organic food each year, but mostly because it costs more. Slightly fewer households now buy organic at all, and price and trust remain the biggest reasons people hold back. Australia's organic boom is a story of land and exports; at the checkout, demand is only just keeping pace.
